Convolution is a binary [[operation]].

_**Definition**_

$$(f*g)(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}{g(t-\tau)f(\tau)d\tau}$$

---

**Remark** Students who have ever taken courses in signal processing or so knows that $(f_g)=(g_f)$, and that the Fourier transform of convolution becomes multiplication (aka. convolution theorem).

It is very easy to prove the aformentioned from a pure algebraic stance. For example, $(f_g)(t)=\int g(t-\tau)f(\tau)d\tau=\int g(t-(t-\tau'))f(t-\tau')|\frac{d\tau}{d\tau'}|d\tau'=\int f(t-\tau')g(\tau')d\tau'=(g_f)(t).$ However, we know nothing about the meaning (physical/geometrical) of the definition or the theorems, we don't know when to use these, and we gain no insight.

To deal with the problem, I would like to start from a more general case, namely Fredholm integral equation of the first kind (notations reused): $$g(s)=\int K(s,t)f(t)dt$$ ($K$ is called a kernel.)

What does the equation mean? Consider a scenario where there is a earthquake source at some point $t=a$, what will we feel at some $s=b$? Say the amplitude of the earthquake at $t$ is $f(t)$, we can model our feeling at $s$ as $g(s)$ by integral equation; that is, $K$ is the effective coefficients that model how the wave propagate in the crust. To be more precise, $K(s,t)$ measures $g(s)$ from a **unit point source** at $t.$ Apart from the above example, we can also think of $f(t)$ as sounds you make at some time $t$, $K$ as the effect of the surrounding valley, and $g(s)$ as the echo you hear at time $s.$ Keep these physical model in mind.

Incidentally, when looking at $K$ along, we can either take the stance of an observer, i.e. for any given $s$, $K(s,t)$ represents the contribution of every unit point source at $t$. By contrast, taking the stance of a source, at any given $t$, the contribution of the unit point source at $t$ to the whole "space" $s$ is the "field" $K(s,t)$. Note that the two intepretation share the same $K$. More importantly, we always integrate across the sources, which is really too reasonable unless you want to integrate across the observation points, which makes no sense at all. (⚠ This last claim is wrong: integrating across the observation points gives the adjoint operator, which is meaningful and widely used. See **Note: Integrating across observers**.)

It is clear that the integral equation occurs in many scenarios, and the next natural question is what is convolution, namely, when can we write $K(s,t)=k(s-t)$? It turns out that it is actually a quite general "special case". Writing $K(s,t)=k(s-t)=k(r)$ means that only the relative "distance" $r$ between $s$ and $t$ matters. Mathematically, this is called shift invariant (see Toeplitz matrix); physically, it means that doing an experiment here or there, now or then, doesn't matter. Note that integration is linear, so by modeling with the integral equation, we implicitly assume that the system is linear. That is to say, if we require a system to be homogeneous (here in the physical sense: uniform space or medium; in linear-systems language "homogeneity" instead means scaling, $L(af)=aL(f)$, which is part of linearity) and assume superposition (linear time invariant (LTI) system), the system kernel is then compressed to $k(s-t)$, and convolution emerge naturally and inevitably (it can be proved mathematically; see also [[Green's function]] and [[impulse response]]). You may want to ask when does a system fail to follow these properties. Actually, it is quite common. Recall our valley example, it is obvious that the valley is spatially inhomogeneous; therefore, if we define $t,s$ as space instead of time, the system is obviously not shift invariant. In plain English, the sound wave reflects to different direction if we speak from different initial position in the valley.

In signal processing, we were taught that convolution is calculated by mirroring $k$, integrating with $f$, shifting $k$ for $s$, and repeating the process for all $s$. At first glance, it seems reasonable to shift $k$ since we need to gather contribution from different sources, but it is not clear why do we need to mirror $k$. The $(s-t)$ is actually quite confusing and difficult for us to wrap our head around. The key is to notice that our final function is actually of variable $s$, and the integration is along $t$. That is to say, we should take the stance of observing from a fix point $s$, i.e. we should think of $s$ as a constant. Now, recall what $k(r)$ means physically, it is simply the response of a unit point source (with the origin centered at the location of the source), and $k(r)=k(s-t)=K(s,t)$. Since we want to integrate along $t$, we fix $s$. In this sense, $k(s-t)=k(r)$ means that we need $r$ that satisfy $r+t=s$ for a given $s$ as $t$ varies, and the integration read as integrating $k(r)f(t)dt$ so long as $r+t$ equals some $s$. In fact, we can write $$g(s)=\int k(s-t)f(t)dt=\int \delta(s-(t+r))k(r)f(t)drdt$$where $\delta$ is the [[Dirac delta function]], and it serves as sift, which is a common trick to denote what should be kept in the domain. (This $\delta$ form is also how convolution appears in probability: the density of a sum $s=t+r$ of independent random variables is $p_S(s)=\int\delta(s-t-r)p_T(t)p_R(r),dt,dr$. Historically, probability is one root of convolution, not the only one: integrals of this form appear in d'Alembert, Lacroix, Laplace, Fourier and Poisson, in several different contexts.) Despite the fact that this is more of a algebraic intepretation, it is actually the exact physical process. The mirroring operation simply stems from the fact that for any fix $s$ (shift), $t$ and $r$ should have different orientation (the addition is capped by $s$ for different $s$ (shift)). To conclude, convolution of two functions means to take one function to be the source distribution and the other to be the unit point source response, and observe the contribution of the entire source distribution from some point $s$, where the system is shift invariant/homogeneous.

People often intepret the operation as a moving average (moving inner prodcut, to be honest) or sliding window; however, a sliding window should actually be $(k\star f)(s)=g(s)=\int \overline{k(t-s)}f(t)dt$ , which is actually called a cross-correlation (or matched filter in signal processing). Although they look similar, they are actually fundamentally different. Cross-correlation measures similarity, and it is built from an inner product; convolution is an algebraic product, which describes the output of an LTI system. (⚠ "Fundamentally different" is wrong as a statement about systems: every cross-correlation is a convolution with a reflected kernel, so a matched filter is itself an LTI system. The real difference is in their algebra as binary operations.)

With $t$ and $r$ as two axes and the constraint $t+r=s$, the rest of the theory is almost free. Since $t$ and $r$ are just the two summands of $s$, nothing in the constraint $t+r=s$ distinguishes them. Swapping their roles means calling $f$ the response and $k$ the source instead, and the integral is unchanged. So $f\ast k=k\ast f$, purely because addition is commutative. Same argument with three axes: $s=t+r+u$. Grouping $(t+r)+u$ or $t+(r+u)$ describes the same set of points, so $(f\ast k)\ast h=f\ast(k\ast h)$. Now ask a different question: is there any input whose _shape_ the system cannot change? Feed in $f(t)=e^{i\omega t}$ and see:  
$$g(s)=\int k(s-t)e^{i\omega t}dt = e^{i\omega s}\int k(r)e^{-i\omega r}dr = \hat k_{\omega}e^{i\omega s}.$$ The exponential comes out unchanged except for one complex number attached to it (eigenvector). Write $\hat k(\omega):=\int k(r)e^{-i\omega r}dr$ — this is the Fourier transform of $k$, called the transfer function or frequency response of the system. This is the whole secret, and it is worth saying slowly. Every shift-invariant system, no matter how complicated its kernel, acts on $e^{i\omega t}$ by simple multiplication. A shift-invariant system can never turn one frequency into another; it can only rescale and delay each one. So if we decompose an arbitrary input into exponentials — that is what the Fourier transform does — the system's action becomes: multiply each component by its own number. This is the convolution theorem,  
$$\widehat{f_k}(\omega)=\hat f(\omega)\hat k(\omega),$$  
and in matrix language it is diagonalization. Convolution in $t$ is a full, entangled integral; in $\omega$ it is pointwise multiplication. You can also see it directly from our two-axis picture, with no talk of frequency at all. The reason $e^{-i\omega s}$ is the right weight is that it converts addition of coordinates into multiplication of values: $e^{-i\omega(t+r)}=e^{-i\omega t}e^{-i\omega r}$. So the constraint $t+r=s$ dissolves and the double integral factors:  
$$\widehat{f_k}(\omega)=\iiint \delta(s-t-r)f(t)k(r)e^{-i\omega s},dt,dr,ds=\Big(\int f e^{-i\omega t}dt\Big)\Big(\int k e^{-i\omega r}dr\Big).$$ And now the two loop back together. Because every LTI system is diagonal in the same basis, cascading two of them means multiplying two diagonal operators — and diagonal entries are plain numbers, which commute. So chaining system $k_1$ then $k_2$ gives exactly the same output as $k_2$ then $k_1$. That is a real physical claim, and not an obvious one: a room's reverberation followed by a loudspeaker's response equals the loudspeaker followed by the room. What breaks it in a real lab is never the algebra — it is clipping, saturation, or a component that drifts with time, i.e. the failure of linearity or of shift invariance. There is one more failure, very common in RF: loading (impedance mismatch). A block's response is measured with a specific source and load impedance. When two blocks are connected, each one loads the other, so the connected system is a different system, not the composition of the two separately measured operators. Swapping the order changes the loading, so the result changes, even though everything is linear and time invariant. This is why two-port cascades multiply ABCD (or T) matrices, which are $2\times2$ matrices at each frequency and do not commute in general. Commutativity returns only when each block is effectively one-way: matched ports, buffers, isolators. So commutativity and the convolution theorem are not two separate facts. They are two readings of the same structure: the system is diagonal in the exponentials. The theorem is the sharper reading, since it also tells you which number sits on the diagonal.

> **Supplement, for later (group theory).** The deeper reason is that the shifts themselves commute. "Shift invariant" means the operator commutes with all translations, and translations on $\mathbb{R}^n$ form an **abelian group** under addition. The general result: for a locally compact group $G$, the convolution algebra $L^1(G)$ is commutative **iff** $G$ is abelian. The exponentials $e^{i\omega x}$ are the **characters** of that group — the functions turning group addition into multiplication — and they are what makes diagonalization possible.
> 
> Change the group and everything reorganizes. Convolution over rotations $SO(3)$ is _not_ commutative (this is why spherical CNNs and crystal texture analysis are harder than the 1-D case); there the Fourier transform is replaced by the **Peter–Weyl theorem**, and the "diagonal entries" become matrix blocks, which do not commute. Take the affine group of scalings-and-shifts, also non-abelian, and the natural transform is the **wavelet transform** instead. Keywords to look up: _character, Pontryagin duality, Peter–Weyl, Toeplitz and circulant matrices_.
> 
> Finite version, if you prefer matrices to integrals: discrete convolution is multiplication by a **Toeplitz matrix**; with periodic boundaries it is a **circulant matrix**, and the DFT matrix diagonalizes _every_ circulant matrix simultaneously. That is the convolution theorem in finite dimensions, and it is fully rigorous with no analysis subtleties.
> 
> One honest caveat: on $\mathbb{R}$ this is not literal diagonalization, because $e^{i\omega t}$ is not square-integrable and so is not an eigenvector in $L^2$. The precise statement is that the Fourier transform turns convolution into a multiplication operator; the spectrum is continuous.

**Note** Causality can also be easily understood. Shift invariance alone allows $k(r)\neq 0$ for $r<0$, i.e. a response arriving before the source acts. Causality is an extra physical postulate:  
$$k(r)=0\ \text{ for } r<0 .$$  
It cuts the line $t+r=s$ in half, leaving $t\le s$:  
$$g(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{s}k(s-t)f(t)dt .$$  
The observer at $s$ collects only from the source's past. This is also why the flipped kernel, anchored at $s$, hangs backwards over the part of $f$ that has already happened. In PDE language this $k$ is the retarded Green's function; in signal processing, a causal filter.

**Note: Integrating across observers** Earlier I claimed that integrating across the observation points "makes no sense at all". It is wrong, and the reason is worth keeping.

Use the inner product $\langle u,v\rangle=\int u,\overline{v}$. For the forward operator $(Kf)(s)=\int K(s,t)f(t),dt$ and any function $h(s)$ on the observation space, $$\langle Kf,h\rangle_s=\iint K(s,t)f(t)\overline{h(s)},dt,ds=\Big\langle f,\ \int\overline{K(s,t)},h(s),ds\Big\rangle_t .$$ So the operator $$(K^*h)(t):=\int\overline{K(s,t)},h(s),ds$$ integrates across the observers. It is the **adjoint** of $K$. In matrix language, $Kf$ sums along a row of $K$ and $K^*h$ sums along a column (it is $K^H h$).

_Why I missed it._ I read the integral equation only as the forward physical story: cause flows from sources to observers, so we sum over sources. The adjoint answers the reverse question: given values $h(s)$ at the observers, how much does each source position $t$ account for them? It sends data back from observers to sources through the same coefficients. My own two stances above already contained this. The "observer" stance is a row of $K$; the "field" stance is a column. Weighting a column by observed data and summing it is exactly $K^*$.

_Where it is used._

- **Inversion.** To solve $g=Kf$ by least squares, minimize $\tfrac12|Kf-g|^2$. The gradient is $K^*(Kf-g)$, so every gradient-based inversion applies the adjoint. In seismology this is the adjoint-state method (full-waveform inversion), the inverse of the earthquake example. Backpropagation in neural networks is the same idea.
- **Imaging.** Backprojection forms the image $\tilde f=K^*g=K^*Kf$. The kernel of $K^*K$, $\ \int\overline{K(s,t)}K(s,t'),ds$, says how a point source at $t'$ appears in the image at $t$: it is the point spread function of the imaging system. The image is exact only when $K^*K=I$.
- **Physical realization.** In a reciprocal medium, $K(s,t)=K(t,s)$ (Lorentz reciprocity; for EM fields the Green dyadic is transposed). So integrating over observers with $K$ is the same as the forward problem with sources and observers swapped. In the frequency domain the remaining complex conjugate is time reversal in the time domain (for real signals). Together: record at the receivers, time-reverse, and re-emit from the receivers, and the waves refocus on the original source. This is the time-reversal mirror (Fink). It needs a lossless, reciprocal medium; loss and non-reciprocal materials (ferrites, magnetized plasma) break it. For antennas, reciprocity is why the transmit and receive patterns are equal.

For a convolution kernel $K(s,t)=k(s-t)$, the adjoint is $$(K^*h)(t)=\int\overline{k(s-t)},h(s),ds=(k\star h)(t),$$ i.e. the **adjoint of convolution is cross-correlation**. This links to the next note.

**Note: Convolution vs cross-correlation** Earlier I wrote that they are "fundamentally different": convolution is an algebraic product describing an LTI system, and cross-correlation is a similarity measure built from an inner product. As a statement about systems, this is wrong.

_Same systems._ Define the reflected conjugate $\tilde k(r):=\overline{k(-r)}$. Then $\tilde k(s-t)=\overline{k(t-s)}$, so $$(k\star f)(s)=\int\overline{k(t-s)}f(t),dt=(\tilde k\ast f)(s).$$ Every cross-correlation is a convolution with another kernel, and the reverse is also true. With $k$ fixed and $f$ as the input, the two describe exactly the same class of LTI systems. The matched filter for template $k$ is the LTI filter with impulse response $\tilde k$.

_Both are inner products._ At each $s$, both outputs are an inner product of $f$ with a shifted template: $$(k\star f)(s)=\langle f,\ k(\cdot-s)\rangle,\qquad (k\ast f)(s)=\langle f,\ \overline{k(s-\cdot)}\rangle=\langle f,\ \tilde k(\cdot-s)\rangle.$$ Correlation compares $f$ with shifted copies of $k$; convolution compares $f$ with shifted copies of $\tilde k$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $|\langle f,k(\cdot-s)\rangle|\le|f|,|k|$, with equality iff $f$ is proportional to $k(\cdot-s)$. This is why correlation measures similarity, and why the matched filter maximizes output SNR in white noise. So "similarity vs. system response" is a difference of interpretation: it depends on which function we call the template.

_What really differs._ The algebra, when both functions are treated as variables in a binary operation:

- Convolution is commutative and associative (the two-axis argument above).
- Correlation is not commutative: $(k\star f)(s)=\overline{(f\star k)(-s)}$. It is not associative in general either.
- In the Fourier domain: $\widehat{k\ast f}=\hat k\hat f$, but $\widehat{k\star f}=\overline{\hat k},\hat f$. The conjugate is exactly what breaks the symmetry. For $k=f$ this gives $\widehat{f\star f}=|\hat f|^2$ (Wiener–Khinchin: autocorrelation ↔ power spectrum).

_The two roles._ From the previous note, correlation with $k$ is the adjoint of convolution with $k$. So convolution with $k$ is the forward model: push a source through the system $k$. Correlation with $k$ is the adjoint: ask how much of $k$'s signature is present in the data. Detection, matched filtering and backprojection are all this adjoint step.

_Why I made the mistake._ I classified the two operations by their use (system response vs. detection), not by their structure. A flip and a conjugate only relabel the kernel, so they cannot change the class of systems. They do change the algebra of the binary operation, and that is the real difference. The sliding-window claim above is still correct: the sliding-window picture literally describes correlation, and it describes convolution only after flipping the kernel.

**Note**

> PyTorch's `Conv2d`, TensorFlow's `conv2d`, and cuDNN all compute  
> $$g(s)=\sum_t k(t-s),f(t)$$  
> with no kernel flip. PyTorch's own documentation says the operation is "more correctly described as cross-correlation." The name is historical inertia.
> 
> Does it matter? For the forward pass of a trained net, no. The kernel is learned, so whatever the optimum is under one convention, its mirror image is the optimum under the other. The function class is identical.
> 
> It matters in three places:
> 
> 1. **Comparing to signal-processing theory.** If you want to apply the convolution theorem, you must flip — or equivalently use $\overline{\hat k(\omega)}$, since $\widehat{k(-\cdot)}=\overline{\hat k}$ for real $k$. FFT-based conv implementations do exactly this internally. Getting it backwards silently mirrors your filters.
> 2. **Backprop.** The gradient w.r.t. the input of a cross-correlation _is_ a true convolution, and vice versa. The flip doesn't disappear; it just moves to the backward pass. This is why `ConvTranspose2d` exists and why it's misnamed "deconvolution."
> 3. **Hand-designed kernels.** If you hard-code a Sobel or Gabor filter from a textbook and feed it to `Conv2d`, you get the mirrored filter. Usually harmless, sometimes not (asymmetric edge detectors, oriented filter banks).
> 
> The misconception worth fixing. CNNs are often said to be "shift invariant." They are shift equivariant: shift the input, and the feature map shifts by the same amount — $T_a(k\star f) = k\star(T_a f)$, not $k\star(T_a f)=k\star f$. Invariance is only approximated later, by global pooling or by the classifier head.
> 
> And even the equivariance is broken in practice. Striding and max-pooling alias, so shifting an input by one pixel can change the output non-trivially; Azulay & Weiss (2019) showed classifier outputs swinging wildly under one-pixel shifts, and Zhang's "Making Convolutional Networks Shift-Invariant Again" (ICML 2019) fixes it by low-pass filtering before subsampling — literally the Nyquist sampling theorem applied to a network. Padding at the borders breaks it too, which is how networks can implicitly encode absolute position.
> 
> One more thing a practitioner would flag. A conv layer is only a convolution along the _spatial_ axes. Across channels it is a full dense contraction:  
> $$g_{c'}(s)=\sum_c \sum_t k_{c'c}(t-s),f_c(t).$$  
> So the layer is shift-equivariant in space and an unconstrained linear map in channels. A $1\times1$ convolution has no spatial extent at all — it's a per-pixel matrix multiply. Grouped and depthwise convolutions are just restrictions on that channel matrix, not changes to the convolution structure.
> 
> Convolution is an LTI system response while cross-correlation is a similarity measure built from an inner product. CNNs want the second — template matching — which is arguably why the unflipped version felt natural to implementers. The irony is that the field kept the other name.
> 
> _(⚠ The first sentence of this paragraph repeats the "fundamentally different" error. See **Note: Convolution vs cross-correlation**.)_
